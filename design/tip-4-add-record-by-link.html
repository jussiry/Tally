Summary

A user can invite someone who isn't on Tally yet as a friend, send them a record, or request one from them, with a link. The user shares the link any way they like — the phone's share sheet, a chat, a copied link — with their own words around it. Opening the link, on a new or an existing account, makes the two friends and, for a record sent or requested, adds the record.

No server takes part beyond the usual relays, no temporary keypair is needed, and every record is signed by the person who owes.

Motivation

A record otherwise needs both people on Tally and connected first. That misses the most common moment — "I owe you, let me note it down", or "you owe me for the tickets" — when the other person isn't on Tally yet. A link fits how people already share things, and it is more personal than a generated email: the sender writes their own message.

An earlier draft sent the link by email through a Tally-run email service. Sharing the link directly is simpler, needs no server and no third-party email provider, and doesn't hand the link's secret to anyone else.

Key idea: a secret that proves "this link was for me"

The author picks a random secret s (32 bytes, base64url) and keeps only h = SHA-256(s) as the way to recognise it. s travels only in the link. The person who opens it proves the link was for them by sending s back in a friend request signed by their own key, so the author's client can accept that request without asking.

Only the person who owes signs a promise. For a record sent, that is the author, and their client sends the record once the link is opened. For a request, the link itself promises nothing: the opener agrees to the amount and their client signs the record.

The link

https://app.tally.earth/#claim?x=friend|send|request&c=<link id>&k=<author npub>&s=<secret>&n=<author name>&t=<opener name>&a=<amount>&m=<note>&r=<relay>…

Everything is in the URL fragment, which browsers don't send to the server, so the secret stays out of server logs. For a request, a and m are what the opener signs, and c names the request so the author's client can close it. For a record sent they only let the welcome screen say what is coming. An invitation has neither (js/utils/claim-link.js).

Author: creating a link

Record by link is reached from Add a friend (opening on Friend), Send and Request ("Not on Tally yet?"). The user picks Friend, Send or Request, enters a name — plus the amount and a note for a record — and gets a link to share. The client writes a signed, self-addressed ledger entry (js/peer/self-entry.js), which reaches the author's other devices through self-sync and never anyone else:

offline_claim (from = to = author) {
  claim_id, direction: "friend" | "send" | "request", commitment: SHA-256(s), secret: s,
  amount_eur, note, recipient_name
}

The secret is kept so the link can be shared again from any of the author's devices. The page lists open links with Share, Copy and Withdraw; withdrawing writes a self-addressed offline_claim_revoked. A link is open until a transaction_created carrying its claim_id exists, in either direction; an invitation closes with an offline_claim_revoked once it is used (getOpenOfflineClaims in js/ledger.ts). Open links don't count in the tally.

Opener: opening a link

The app takes the link out of the URL at once (js/app.js), then:

  • No account in this browser: the welcome screen says "Alice invites you to Tally", "Alice recorded €20 to you" or "Alice asks you to record €20 to them", shows the note, suggests the name Alice used, and offers "Create a user and claim" or "Create a user and accept". Logging in with an existing key works too.
  • An account exists, invitation or record sent: it goes ahead at once; a record is added to that account.
  • An account exists, request: the user is asked first ("If you accept, you sign a record that you owe Alice €20"), since accepting is a promise.

Then claimFromLink (js/commands/offline-claim.js) sends the author a friend_request whose payload carries claim_proof: s. If the two are already friends or a request is pending, the proof goes in a request of its own. For an accepted request, the opener's client also writes a self-addressed link_payment entry (claim_id, friend, amount, note) — or, when the two are already friends, sends the record straight away.

Author's client: accepting

In applyInboundFriendRequest (js/peer/handlers.js), a request whose SHA-256(claim_proof) matches an open link is handled with no prompt: the friendship is accepted, if it isn't already.

  • Invitation: nothing more; the invitation is used up.
  • Record sent: the author's client sends the record.
  • Request: nothing more. When the opener's client sees the accept (applyInboundFriendAccept), it sends the record for each open link_payment to that friend.

Either way the record is an ordinary transaction_created with transaction_id = "tx_" + claim_id and the claim_id, so it passes the usual "accepted friends only" rule, closes the link, and is applied once even if two devices of the same user both send it. A deactivated friendship is left alone.

Leaked links

The secret is a bearer token: whoever holds the link can open it. The person sharing it decides the channel, and nothing else sees it.

  • A record sent could be claimed by the wrong person. The author is told who claimed it ("Erik claimed the €20 you recorded").
  • A request could be accepted by the wrong person, who then owes the author. Harmless to the author.
  • An invitation could make the author friends with the wrong person, who shows up by their own chosen name. The author can deactivate the friendship.

Open questions

  1. Confirm claims by hand? A setting to approve each claim of a record sent before it goes out.
  2. Expiry. Should links expire after N days, shortening the window a leaked link is useful?
  3. Used links. Opening a link that was already used or withdrawn sends a request the author ignores, and the opener isn't told. The author's client could answer with a short "not available" message.
  4. Several devices of the opener. The link is opened on one device; the others learn of the friendship and record through self-sync.
