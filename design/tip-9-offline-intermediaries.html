Summary

TIP-7 v1 needs every participant of a routed payment online, because each one signs. This TIP lets an intermediary be offline by signing their consent in advance: a routing budget — "while I can't be reached, up to €X may be routed from Alice onward through me". The budget is a binding promise, separate from the informal trust limit, opt-in, consumed by use and never self-renewing. The upstream neighbour, who is online, keeps its running total; overspending it is provable fraud, and the intermediary's exposure can never exceed the budget they chose.

The example used throughout

Alice wants to pay Carol €20 for a lamp. They are not friends, but both are friends with Bob, so the payment is routed through him:

Alice ──IOU €20──> Bob ──IOU €20──> Carol
payer            intermediary        payee

Afterwards Alice owes Bob €20 more, Bob owes Carol €20 more, and Bob is even. The difference from TIP-7 is that Bob is offline: his phone is off, so he cannot approve the payment when it happens. Alice and Carol are online.

What Bob risks is the first link: he now holds Alice's IOU, and if she never pays it, he still owes Carol. So the question this TIP answers is how Bob can agree in advance, and how much.

Motivation

A routed payment succeeds only if every intermediary approves it immediately. People's phones are off, out of signal, or in a drawer; most of the time a route of three friends will have at least one who can't answer. Service-worker signing (push wakes the app, which signs with its local key) covers a device that has network but a closed app. It cannot cover a device that is unreachable, and nothing can sign there without the key. The only way to take part while unreachable is to have agreed beforehand.

That is what a credit line was in RipplePay: standing consent to be routed through. Tally cannot copy it directly, because RipplePay had a server that knew every balance and could stop a revoked line instantly. Here there is no such authority, which shapes everything below.

Why not make the trust limit binding

The obvious design is to make the existing trust limit the promise: Bob's limit for Alice is standing consent to be routed through up to that limit. It fails on mistakes and on revocation:

  • A trust limit refills. Capacity is limit minus current debt, so every time Alice's debt to Bob shrinks, the promise is available again. It has no end.
  • A withdrawal can't be proven received. Bob can lower or cancel the limit, but if he is unreachable and Alice claims she never received the change, nobody can show otherwise — there is no shared clock and no authority. So whatever the limit says when Bob goes offline is effectively promised.
  • The damage lands on Bob, not on the person who misbehaved. Bob ends up owed by Alice and owing Carol, who did nothing wrong. Unlike a direct IOU, where Bob was party to the deal and can refuse or renegotiate if it went bad, a routed IOU is a debt Bob owes for a deal he had no part in. If Alice never pays, Bob still owes Carol.

Combine the three and an accidental million-euro limit is a million-euro liability that cannot be withdrawn. So the trust limit stays what it is today — informal, governing what you approve while you are online — and the binding promise becomes a separate, much smaller number.

The routing budget

Only used when Bob is offline. When Bob can be reached — app open, or woken by a push — he approves each payment live, exactly as in TIP-7, and can approve anything up to his full trust limit with Alice. The routing budget is consulted only when nobody can ask him. It is a second, smaller number for the one situation where Bob cannot say no.

RoutingBudget {               // durable; authored by the intermediary (Bob)
  id:        "rb_…"
  from:      Bob              // who is promising
  upstream:  Alice            // whose IOUs Bob accepts while unreachable
  amount:    50.00            // ceiling on the running total; see §Withdrawing it
  replaces:  "rb_…" | null    // the grant this one supersedes
  after_use: "ru_…" | null    // the last use of the replaced grant Bob had seen
}
  • Per upstream friend. Bob grants Alice €50 and another friend, Dave, €30, separately. His total exposure is the sum, which he can see.
  • Consumed, not a ceiling. Each routed payment uses part of the budget; nothing gives it back. When it is spent, Bob must come back online and grant again. An ignored withdrawal can therefore gain at most the remaining budget, once.
  • Downstream is open. Bob's IOU may go to any of his friends who accepts it. Owing them more costs Bob nothing — he holds Alice's IOU for the same amount — and each of them signs for themselves.
  • Also bounded by the trust limit. A use must fit both the remaining budget and Bob's current headroom with Alice computed from their shared ledger.
  • Opt-in, default zero.

In the UI: the "offline limit"

Users should not need the term "routing budget". It appears as a second field on the existing trust-limit page for a friend, under the trust limit itself:

Trust limit: €200

Offline limit: €20

While you're offline, others can pay through you up to this amount without asking you. Keep it smaller than your trust limit: you will owe it to the person paid even if Alice never pays you back, and a change you make while offline only takes effect once Alice receives it.

  • It can never exceed the trust limit, and the UI suggests a fraction of it.
  • It shows what is left ("€12 of €20 left") and when it was last used, since it doesn't refill on its own.
  • Saving the field grants a new budget; setting it to zero withdraws it.

Withdrawing it

Bob withdraws or lowers a budget by granting a new one that names the grant it replaces (amount: 0 to withdraw). It takes effect when Alice receives it. There are no dates: without a shared clock, an expiry time would be stated by whoever uses the offer, and so could not be enforced.

One running total per lineage. A replacing grant does not start a fresh budget. Uses of the old grant and the new one count against one running total, and amount is a ceiling on that total. So lowering €50 to €20 when €35 is already spent leaves nothing to spend, rather than €20 more. Without this, Alice could spend the old grant in full and then the new one too — lowering a budget would raise the exposure.

The race. Uses Alice made before she received the replacement are valid under the old ceiling, because in her view the promise was still in force. Uses after it must fit the new one. Alice cannot be shown to have received it late, so the worst case is that the old ceiling applies in full — but never more than the largest ceiling Bob ever granted. That is Bob's maximum loss, and he chose it. after_use records the last use Bob had seen when he replaced the grant, so both sides can tell which uses he knew about.

Using a budget

Alice, the upstream neighbour, is the only one who can spend a budget Bob granted her, so she is the natural one to keep its running total. Each use is a small record Alice signs:

RoutingBudgetUse {            // authored by the upstream neighbour (Alice)
  id:        "ru_…"
  budget:    "rb_…"
  previous:  "ru_…" | null    // the previous use of this budget
  amount:    20.00
  spent:     35.00            // running total including this use
  payment:   "rpay_…"         // the routed payment it backs
}

In the routed payment record, Bob's leg carries this use in place of Bob's live signature. The record is valid if every online participant signed it and every offline intermediary's leg carries a use that fits its budget.

Overspending is provable. Uses form a single line: each names the one before it. If Alice issues two uses naming the same previous, she has signed two contradictory accounts of the same budget, and both are evidence against her. Bob is liable only for uses that fit the budget in order. Whoever accepted an IOU from Bob beyond that holds Alice's signed contradiction as their claim against her — see §Open questions for whether downstream friends should be able to limit that risk.

Bob learns everything when he returns. Every routed payment is a multi-party record that downstream friends also hold, so Bob receives all of them by ordinary sync, whether or not Alice delivers them, and can check the running total himself.

Who may be offline

Every leg of a route moves risk to the person who receives the IOU: on the leg Alice → Bob, Bob now holds Alice's IOU. That person must either sign live, or have granted a budget and have the other end of that leg online, to keep the running total. It follows that:

  • The payer is always online. They start the payment and, per TIP-7, sign last.
  • No two neighbours in a route may both be offline, because the leg between them would have nobody to approve it or keep its total.
  • The payee may be offline if the last intermediary is online and the payee has granted that intermediary a budget: they can be paid while away, which is the same promise in the other position.

A three-person route therefore works with the middle person offline; a longer route works with every other person offline.

Disputes

A routed payment is not one deal but a route of IOUs, and only its two ends made the deal. If the lamp Carol sold Alice turns out to be broken, that is between Alice and Carol. A refund is a new routed payment in the other direction, with the same consent rules; no intermediary is ever obliged to reverse anything, because none of them was party to the sale.

That is also why an intermediary's own risk must stay small and chosen: if Alice refuses to pay Bob, Bob's IOU to Carol still stands. A routing budget is the most Bob agrees to lose that way while he cannot be asked.

Interaction with other TIPs

  • TIP-7. Validation rule 1 (every participant signed) becomes "every online participant signed, and each offline one is covered by a budget use". Payer-signs-last is unchanged. No in-flight reservation is needed on a budget-backed leg: Alice is the only writer of its running total.
  • TIP-8. Budgets and their uses are entries between Alice and Bob. Uses are not debt, so a checkpoint does not include them, but the debt they create is covered like any other. The current grant and its latest use stay live across a checkpoint; earlier grants and uses can then be archived.
  • Service-worker signing (push wakes the app, which signs with its local key) is the better path whenever the intermediary's device has network: it approves each payment live, so it needs no budget. Budgets are for when that fails.

Open questions

  1. Downstream protection. Carol accepts Bob's IOU on the strength of Alice's running total, which Carol cannot check. Should each friend be able to set a separate cap on how much they accept through offline-backed legs?
  2. Equivocation policy. When Alice issues two uses that conflict, which branch does Bob honour? Order by lowest id is deterministic; is it fair?
  3. Defaults and UI. How small should the suggested budget be, and how should the cost of a broken promise be explained so it isn't granted carelessly?
  4. Renewal. Should Bob's app top up a budget automatically when he comes back online, within a ceiling he set once?
  5. Visibility. Should Alice see how much of Bob's budget is left, and should Bob be notified each time his budget is used?
  6. Fees. If intermediaries take fees (TIP-7 open question 3), does an offline intermediary's fee come from the budget record?
